The World · The Gulf War
The Guns Stay Quiet, and Oman Drafts the Formula
A single held night has hardened into a third day of mutual restraint, and in Tehran an Omani delegation is drafting who runs the Strait of Hormuz. The document exists; the signatures do not. Two regional sources say any deal still needs Trump's approval — which is another way of saying the verdict is not in.
this story

What was a one-night stand-down on Friday is, by Sunday, something closer to a truce that dares not speak its name. The United States held off striking Iran for a second straight day on Sunday, and Tehran said it had done the same, as mediators worked toward an interim ceasefire.1 The venue for the actual bargaining is Muscat's shuttle: rounds of Iranian–Omani deputy-foreign-minister talks in Tehran on Friday and Saturday, focused, in the official phrasing, on "common principles and operational mechanisms" for safe passage — a formula under which Iran would manage transit with fewer restrictions on the ships that pass.2

The delta on last week is real and worth marking precisely: No. 53 recorded a single order not given. This is a pattern of orders not given, plus a text being written. But a drafted mechanism is not a signed one. Iran's foreign minister still reads June's memorandum as granting Tehran the right to set the strait's routes; Washington still insists Iran "does not control" Hormuz.3 The clause that broke the June truce is the exact clause Oman is now trying to word around, and two regional sources cautioned that whatever emerges "requires Trump's approval." The barrel's read on all this is on p15.

The framing from the Gulf press is rights, not concessions: coastal states hold "lawful rights" over the waterway, and the whole crisis traces to a "fatal ambiguity" in the word safe passage that let each side claim the other had breached. Iran is cast as defending a legal entitlement against American coercion, and the talks as a chance for the US to accept what the map already says.4

Beijing — MOFADe-escalation, with an interest

Spokesman Lin Jian's line is that "resuming free and safe passage in the strait at an early date serves the interests of all sides," that "military means cannot solve the fundamental problems," and that Beijing backs "the lawful rights of coastal nations." China is the largest buyer of Iranian crude, so the appeal to open water is also an appeal to its own supply — the self-interest is stated, not hidden.5

The Sceptic

Read the calendar, not the communiqué. The June memorandum also "paused" this war and broke within weeks on this same navigation clause; analysts note prior ceasefires drew mutual violation claims within hours, and the Hormuz risk premium is now treated as permanently higher — Rapidan still models Brent near $100 through 2026. The clean falsifier is simple: if strikes resume before this issue is a day old, the draft was a breather, not a turn. What would change the call is a signed text with a named enforcement mechanism, not another "progress" readout.6

A Refinery in Siberia, and an Outbreak That Passed a Grim Mark
While the Gulf drafted its truce, Ukraine's drones reached two thousand kilometres east again, and Central Africa's Ebola epidemic quietly became the fastest-growing on record.

Ukraine's long-range campaign found western Siberia. On 25 July, Ukrainian drones struck the Antipinsky refinery near Tyumen — more than two thousand kilometres from the front and among the deepest strikes of the war — touching off a major fire.1 It is one node in a campaign that has hit Russian refineries close to two hundred times in the first half of 2026; Kyiv's own claim, that roughly two-fifths of Russia's refining capacity has been knocked offline at one time or another, should be read as a war aim as much as a measurement. The strategic logic is the same as Hormuz's, inverted: both wars are now fought over who can meter the other side's oil.

The graver number is medical. After a provincial data reconciliation, the Bundibugyo-strain Ebola outbreak across the Democratic Republic of Congo and into Uganda rose to some 2,905 confirmed cases and 1,269 deaths as of 22 July — past the thousand-death mark, and now described as the fastest-growing Ebola epidemic on record, with no licensed vaccine or therapeutic for this strain.2 Ituri province accounts for roughly nine in ten cases. It is the kind of story that does not trend because it is neither new nor near, which is precisely the failure the essay at the back of this book is about.

Colombia counts down. President Petro's term ends 7 August; the 21 June run-off pitted the right's Abelardo de la Espriella, who led the first round, against the left's Iván Cepeda. A transition in its final fortnight.3

The demand side softens. With the war premium in the barrel, the IEA and traders both flag weakening physical demand — a glut argument that, if right, means much of the price is fear, not barrels (see p15).4

And the Ebola tally jumped partly because of a data catch-up, not only fresh spread, so the "fastest-growing on record" label is real but partly an artefact of counting. Both numbers are directionally alarming and worth holding loosely on precision.5

Two Verdicts Land Before Lunch
The central bank rules this morning on a quarter that grew 5.7% and an inflation rate still mild — and it does so with a US forced-labour tariff now costed by the manufacturers themselves. The consensus is a hold. The interesting sentence will be the one about what comes next.
this story

The Monetary Authority of Singapore publishes its quarterly statement this morning, and the near-unanimous expectation is that it keeps the Singapore-dollar band exactly where it is — no change to slope, width or centre. A Reuters poll had twelve of sixteen economists calling a hold; June core inflation at 1.6% and headline at 1.9% both sit inside the 1.5–2.5% comfort range, and MAS last tightened only in April.1 By the time most readers open this, the decision will be public. We are holding the outcome rather than pre-printing it: a hold is the base case, not a fact, and this desk does not report a verdict before it is handed down.

Watch the forward language, not the band. The awkward geometry is a backward-looking quarter that ran hot — Q2 growth of 5.7%, June non-oil exports up sharply — sitting against a US tariff that only began to bite on 24 July. Some of that strength is order front-loading ahead of the duty; the question is whether MAS narrows its growth wording even while it holds. On the tariff itself, the weekend delta is that the Singapore Manufacturing Federation is now surveying members' US exposure to size the hit, while the SMF and Singapore Business Federation both reject the forced-labour label the 12.5% duty rests on.2 The read for a portfolio owner is on p15; the point here is civic — no Singapore firm has been named, so the number was set by category, not case.

Turf City Opens Its Book, and Half of It Sells
Dunearn House moved 211 of 380 units at launch — the three-bedders sold out, but 55% is well shy of this year's ~76% norm. Correcting our own held figure from No. 53: the reflexive prime-district bid has a ceiling, and this is the clearest reading of it yet.
this story

A correction first, because keeping honest score is the house rule. No. 53 carried a "~99% take-up" figure for Dunearn House, held pending a named source. The named source is in, and it is materially softer: Bukit Timah Turf City's first condominium sold 211 of its 380 units over the 25–26 July launch weekend — a 55.5% take-up.1 The three-bedroom and three-bed-plus-flexi stock sold out; roughly 73% of the two-bed-plus-study went; prices ran from about S$2,799 per square foot, or S$1.475m for a two-bedder. Strong at the top of the stack, resistant everywhere else.

The tell is the gap to the field. This year's launches have averaged around 76% on opening weekend, with several clearing near 90%; a 5,900-strong preview crowd two weeks ago that converted to 55% is the signature of price resistance, not weak interest.2 Read it as a market verdict rather than a flop: buyers will pay up for genuine scarcity — the own-stay three-bedders — but they baulk at ~S$2,800 psf on a first-mover plot inside a masterplan that is still mostly a drawing, with an untested resale exit in 2030. Prime-district pricing power, in other words, is not infinite, and Dunearn just found the edge of it.

The Biggest Open Model Ever, Unloadable by Design
Moonshot's Kimi K3 — 2.8 trillion parameters — publishes its weights on Hugging Face at eight o'clock this morning, our time. It is the largest open-weights release in history and, for all but a handful of data centres, the least usable. "Open," this week, becomes a thing you can read about but not run.
this story

Two verdicts on Kimi K3 arrive almost together, and they point in opposite directions. The release is real: K3's open weights are scheduled to publish under Moonshot's Hugging Face org at 00:00 UTC — 08:00 in Singapore — the self-host milestone for a model that has been live via API since 16 July.1 We are holding the confirmation that the repository is actually populated at press time; the honest note is that trackers warn readers to trust only the official moonshotai org and not the mirror repos already staking out the name.

The catch is arithmetic. The native release is a quantised MXFP4 package of roughly 594 gigabytes; the full-precision footprint is near 1.4 terabytes, and Moonshot's own deployment guidance assumes supernodes of sixty-four or more accelerators, with a practical floor around eight H100s.2 A single RTX 5090, with its 32 gigabytes, is not within two orders of magnitude of loading it, even at two-bit. The model was trained to be served quantised, which is elegant engineering and cold comfort to anyone hoping to run frontier-scale weights at home. For our reader's home lab, the runnable Moonshot model remains K2.7 Code, not K3 — see the facing page.

The One You Can Actually Run
While the record model stays in the data centre, the home-lab stack keeps its own quiet momentum: a Moonshot code model that fits a single card, a Qwen daily driver, and a frontier price war that finally rewards the buyer.

The useful counterpoint to K3 is what already runs on the reader's bench. Moonshot's own K2.7 Code, in Unsloth's dynamic GGUF, is servable on a single high-VRAM card through llama.cpp with multi-token prediction — a genuinely strong coding model that loads, unlike its trillion-parameter sibling.1 Qwen3 30B remains the sensible everyday brain, and the community's re-quantisation machine — BF16 and GGUF conversions, vLLM and SGLang ports — is already spinning up around K3 for the day someone with a rack wants to try it. The lesson holds from last week: for a home lab, "open and runnable" beats "open and record-breaking" every time.

The frontier above them is finally competing on price. GPT-5.6 "Sol" is expected to reach general availability by month-end; on Artificial Analysis it scores 59 to Fable 5's 60, but Fable still wins SWE-bench Pro by roughly fifteen points while Sol runs at about a third of the cost.2 July 2026 is the most crowded frontier-launch month on record, and for once the crowding lands in the buyer's favour: capability is converging and the price of the second-best is collapsing.

The Timeline No Parent Set
Two new cohorts point the same way: the driver of a preteen's low mood may be the pace and timing of puberty itself, not her screen. Earlier-maturing children — girls and boys — carry higher odds of depression, and a hormone can move before anything shows. General guidance, not medical advice.
this story

The unsettling finding for a parent of an eleven-year-old is that some of the mood may be arriving on a clock nobody set. A Danish population cohort of nearly sixteen thousand children links earlier pubertal timing to higher diagnosed-depression risk: girls with earlier first menstruation around a third more likely, earlier breast development about a quarter more, and — the part that resists the "it's-a-girls-thing" story — boys with earlier development up to two-thirds more likely.1 It is correlational, as these things are, but it is large, and it points at biology and timing rather than behaviour.

A second analysis sharpens the mechanism. Researchers at the University of Georgia, working with more than five thousand girls in the long-running ABCD study, report that the year-over-year change in testosterone across ages ten to twelve predicts rising anxiety and depression symptoms better than visible physical development does — and that the distress can precede any outward sign a parent would notice.2 The lead author is careful: "What we found are not clinical disorders… a developmental precursor or a red flag." The practical translation is about the timing of attention, not alarm — the low-key check-in and the normalising conversation belong before the visible milestones, not after.

What does "attention" look like in practice, short of alarm? It is unremarkable. A standing habit of asking about the day and actually listening; not reading a suddenly-touchy eleven-year-old as a discipline problem; keeping the lines open about mood before there is a reason to. The finding is not a cue to monitor a hormone — it is a case for being present during a window the child cannot see coming and cannot control, so that if the mood does dip, someone she trusts is already in the room.

The Small Lever That Still Works
If the timeline is fixed, the useful question is what a parent can actually move. This week's answer is unglamorous and evidence-backed: the quality of her friendships, and the fact that you can help build it.

Set against a puberty clock you cannot reset, friendship is a lever you can. A small controlled trial of solution-focused group therapy in anxious adolescents reported improved peer-friendship quality — a reminder that the social world, not just the screen, is a place distress is made and unmade, and one a parent can help shape.1 It is a modest study and this desk flags it as such, but it rhymes with the larger literature: warm, low-pressure connection is protective, and the skills that build it can be taught. The practical read for an eleven-year-old is to invest in the one or two real friendships over the wide, shallow feed.

The playbook writes itself from the two pages together. If the biology is on its own schedule, the parent's work is the environment around it: protect sleep, keep the check-ins frequent and unremarkable, normalise talking about mood before there is a crisis, and back the friendships that do the quiet buffering. None of it is a cure and none of it is medical advice; all of it is cheap, repeatable, and within a parent's gift — which, on a week when the headline finding is a timeline no one controls, is the more useful half of the story.

It helps to know why the lever works. A close friendship gives an adolescent a place to co-regulate — to borrow calm from someone steady, to feel that a bad day is survivable because it is shared. That is protective in a way a screen rule is not, because it adds something rather than removing it, and the wellbeing research keeps returning to belonging as the load-bearing variable. None of which means engineering her friendships for her. It means noticing which ones steady her, making the home an easy place for those friends to gather, and treating the social calendar as seriously as the homework one.

Foraging the same fields as honeybees, bumblebees come home carrying several times as much toxic metal — arsenic, cadmium, lead — even in gentle English countryside. Same landscape, same flowers, very different dose. The dinner-party version: not all pollinators sip from the same glass.

Two bees can work the same meadow and come home poisoned to different degrees. A Cambridge study reports that bumblebees foraging the same landscapes as honeybees accumulate between two and seven times more toxic heavy metals — arsenic, cadmium, lead, tin — in their pollen, and roughly three times more in their bodies, even in low-contamination Cambridgeshire.1 The likely reason is habits: bumblebees range differently, visit different flowers, and forage in weather that keeps honeybees home, so they hoover up more of whatever a landscape is quietly carrying.4 It is a neat, counterintuitive fact with a sting — the "clean" countryside is cleaner for some pollinators than others.

The second item is a city that kept its family tree on one side. Put the two together and the through-line is that behaviour, not geography, does the deciding — the bee's foraging habits, the city's marriage rules. Same field, different dose; same era, different destiny by which side of the family you were born on. Neither is a headline about you, and both are the kind of small, exact fact that survives a dinner because it rearranges something you thought you knew.

A Columbia pooled analysis of randomised crossover trials — 95 adults who normally slept seven hours or more, at elevated cardiometabolic risk — cut their sleep by roughly eighty minutes a night for six weeks and watched the scale move: about a pound of weight, half a centimetre of waist, and, tellingly, more than seventeen extra minutes a day spent awake but inactive.1 The mechanism is the story. In the subset measured, moderate-to-vigorous activity and total energy expenditure barely changed — the weight tracked sedentary time, not appetite. Short sleep didn't make them eat; it made them sit.

Because it is a randomised crossover, not an observational snapshot, the usual "confounded" critique lands weaker: the same people were compared to themselves across conditions. For a mid-forties reader optimising with a ring on his finger, the read is that protecting the sleep window may guard non-exercise movement — the incidental walking and fidgeting that quietly burns the day's calories — more than it curbs snacking. The Sealed Room
This week a reader waits on half a dozen verdicts — two central banks, a Gulf transit deal, a film's embargo, a model's release. It is worth asking why so many of our decisions are made behind a locked door, and handed down only when someone decides the waiting is complete. The answer is older than the Fed, and it is not about secrecy. It is about how a decision becomes a verdict.
this story

Consider the shape of the week this issue reports. The Monetary Authority of Singapore deliberates and then, at an appointed hour, releases a statement. The Federal Reserve seals itself in a room on Tuesday and Wednesday and emerges with a sentence the whole world has agreed in advance to treat as law. An Omani delegation drafts a Hormuz formula that will mean nothing until a single man says yes. A studio holds its reviewers under embargo until a clock strikes. A laboratory in Beijing sets its model's weights to publish at midnight, Greenwich time, not a minute before. In each case the decision is, in some sense, already made — and withheld. The withholding is not an accident of process. It is the process.1

We have a deep instinct to lock the decision-makers in. The word for the oldest example is literal: conclave, from the Latin cum clave — "with a key." The cardinals who choose a pope are locked in, and the practice was not a theologian's idea but an exasperated town's. In 1268, at Viterbo, the cardinals dithered so long over a new pope that the townspeople locked them into the papal palace, cut their rations to bread and water, and, when that failed, tore the roof off to hurry the Holy Spirit along. The election that resulted took the better part of three years — the longest in history — and produced Gregory X, who promptly wrote the sealed conclave into church law so that no election would drift again.2

The word is literal. Conclave — cum clave, "with a key." We lock the deciders in, and we always have.

The lesson Viterbo teaches is counter-intuitive. The lock was imposed to speed a decision, but what it produced was something more valuable than speed: a verdict nobody could second-guess, because everyone could see the conditions under which it was reached. The cardinals had no outside pressure to appeal to and no way to blame a leak. When the white smoke rose, the town did not merely learn who. It learned that the decision had been made in the only way that made it binding — sealed, and therefore owned.

The instinct did not stay in the church. Every institution that needs its decisions obeyed rather than merely announced has reached, sooner or later, for the same device: a room, a door, a period of enforced silence, and a fixed moment of release. Courts did it with the jury. Republics did it with the secret ballot. Markets, improbably, did it with the modern central bank. The forms differ; the logic is identical, and it long predates any of them. What follows are three versions of the same machine — one sacred, one legal, one monetary — and what each was built to protect.

The Argument in One Line

The sealed interval is not a delay we tolerate on the way to a decision. It is the machinery that turns a decision into a verdict — one the loser will accept. When we run out of genuine sealed rooms, we build fake ones.

The law borrowed the same device, and made it harsher. For centuries the English common-law jury, once it retired to consider a verdict, was kept together "without meat, drink, fire, or candle" until it agreed — sequestered, deprived, and sealed off from the world precisely so that the verdict, when it came, would be the jury's own and no one else's.1 The discomfort was the point. A verdict reached in a warm room with the doors open and the newspapers arriving would carry no authority, because anyone could imagine it had been got at. A verdict reached by twelve hungry strangers locked in a cold room until they agreed is one even the condemned man's family can be told to respect. The sealing manufactures the legitimacy.

Venice, which trusted no one, industrialised the instinct. The Republic's Council of Ten and its secretive electoral machinery for choosing a Doge were a baroque sequence of sealed ballots and locked chambers, deliberately so convoluted that no faction could rig the outcome — legitimacy engineered through opacity. The city that invented double-entry bookkeeping, where everything must be seen, also invented the most elaborate hidden room in Europe, because it understood that some decisions are trusted precisely because they were made where no one could interfere.2

The discomfort was the point. A verdict got in a warm room with the doors open carries no authority — anyone could imagine it was got at.

This is the thread that runs to the Federal Reserve. The modern central bank is a secular conclave, and it knows it. It observes a "blackout period" before each meeting, during which officials say nothing; it deliberates behind closed doors; it releases its verdict at a fixed hour, in liturgical language, and only later publishes the minutes, as the Vatican eventually publishes an account of the reign. The ritual is not bureaucratic habit. It is the same technology Viterbo stumbled into: a decision made in a sealed room, at an appointed time, is one the markets will treat as authoritative even when they disagree with it. The suspense is not friction in the system. It is the system's way of conferring weight.

The signal matters as much as the seal, which is why every sealed room evolves a way to announce itself. The conclave burns its ballots twice a day — black smoke for no decision, white for a pope — the chemistry now helped along with cartridges and, since 2005, a ringing bell to settle the ambiguous grey that once sent Rome into false celebration.3 The jury has its foreman rising to read a single word; the Fed has its fixed-hour statement and its half-past-two press conference. A sealed room is useless without a trusted interface to the waiting crowd — one unfudgeable moment when the withheld verdict becomes public and binding at once.

Black smoke for no decision, white for a pope. Every sealed room evolves a way to announce that the waiting is over.

There is a second function, quieter and less flattering. The seal protects the deciders. A verdict owned by a locked room is one no single member must answer for alone: the cardinal, the juror, the committee member is shielded by the collective and by the secrecy of the ballot — which is why the same institutions that demand the decision also guard, fiercely, the question of who inside voted which way. The sealing makes the verdict binding on the world and, at the same time, bearable for the people who had to reach it.

From conclave to jury room to the FOMC blackout: the withheld interval is the machinery, not the wait.

Here is where the argument turns, and where it should worry us a little. If the sealed interval is a technology for making legitimacy, then it can be counterfeited — and increasingly it is. The film held under review embargo, the product unveiled at a staged "drop," the model whose weights are set to publish at a ceremonial midnight, the start-up that briefs no one until the appointed hour: each borrows the grammar of the conclave without its substance. The suspense is engineered not to protect a decision from interference, but to concentrate attention and manufacture an event. The white smoke has become a marketing instrument.1

There is nothing wrong with a well-run reveal; anticipation is a legitimate pleasure, and a review embargo protects a critic's independence as surely as a sealed jury protects a verdict. The danger is subtler. When every announcement is dressed as a sealed-room verdict, the reader loses the ability to tell the decisions that were genuinely deliberated from the ones that were merely timed. A central bank's blackout and a phone-maker's countdown look, on the calendar, identical. One is protecting a judgement. The other is protecting a launch window. The skill this asks of a reader is to look past the staging to the thing being staged — to ask, of each sealed room, whether anything is actually being decided inside it.

The counterfeiting has a cost, and it is not only aesthetic. When every announcement borrows the grammar of the verdict, genuine deliberation gets harder to see and, worse, harder to value. A reader trained by a thousand staged "drops" to feel the little jolt of a reveal will feel the same jolt whether a committee agonised for two days or a marketing team picked a Tuesday. The currency of the sealed room — trust earned by visible, constrained deliberation — is debased by overprinting, exactly as a real currency is. The antidote is not cynicism about all reveals; it is a stubborn insistence on asking what, if anything, was weighed.

The white smoke has become a marketing instrument. The skill is telling the deliberated verdict from the merely timed one.

Which returns us to the front of the book, and to William James. To make a choice and not make it, he wrote, is itself a choice. The sealed room is society's way of forcing the choice — of ensuring that the cardinals, the jury, the committee cannot defer forever, and that when they finally speak, the rest of us will treat the answer as settled. This week the reader waits on several such rooms at once. Two of them — the central banks — are the real thing: genuine deliberation, sealed to keep it honest, released to make it binding. Others are closer to theatre. The discipline the essay recommends is the same one the whole book runs on: honour the verdict that was earned behind the locked door, and notice, quietly, when the door was locked on an empty room.

From the Desk

The wager of this issue is small and old: that the waiting is not the boring part before the news, but the part that makes the news mean something. When the Fed's sentence lands on Wednesday, or MAS's this morning, the weight it carries was made in the days it made you wait. Spend the week, then, not resenting the sealed rooms, but sorting them — the ones deciding something from the ones merely staging it.

The reward is not foresight about a rate decision. It is a habit of attention: the reader who can tell a conclave from a countdown is harder to sell to, and better briefed, than the one who treats every timed release as an event of equal weight. That, in the end, is what a briefing is for — not to tell you the verdict a moment sooner, but to tell you which verdicts were worth the wait.
