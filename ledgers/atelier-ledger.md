|Claw machines (the arcade kind)|2|**The most honest machine in the arcade is the one everybody calls rigged — and both halves of that sentence are true.** The desk's job: separate the ENGINEERING from the payout logic. Start with the mechanism — three-prong vs two-prong claws, gantry rails and cable-drum lifts, the difference between a machine that is *set* to be hard and one *built* to be skilful. The central technical fact: most Western machines run a **payout-percentage** model — the claw is given full grip strength only every Nth play, which is a slot machine wearing a crane costume — while Japanese **UFO Catcher**-lineage machines are typically tuned for *skill* play (single-axis two-button placement, and the 橋渡し / hashiwatashi "bridge" settings where the goal is to topple or slide a prize rather than lift it, so a good player genuinely wins more). That distinction is the whole article. Makers and tiers: **Sega** (the UFO Catcher line since 1985 — the genre's Leica) and **Taito** (Clena) at the top of the Japanese bench, **Bandai Namco** and Komuse alongside; the Western commercial trade led by **Elaut** (Belgium), **Smart Industries** (Iowa, in the business since the 1920s), **ICE** and Coast to Coast. White-label truth: the desktop "mini claw" flood and much of the budget commercial tier are the same Guangdong OEM chassis under a dozen badges — and even at the commercial end, cabinet art is the differentiator far more often than the movement. Price the ladder honestly: toy/desktop from ~S$100 (a novelty, not a machine); grey-import used Japanese cabinets from Yahoo Auctions via proxy (roughly ¥150,000–400,000 before freight); new Western commercial units in the US$5,000–8,000 band. The buy-once engineering notes that matter for a home in Singapore: **voltage and frequency** (Japanese cabinets are 100V — a step-down transformer, not an adapter, and check the 50/60Hz spec), physical footprint and lift access to an HDB/condo unit, parts availability and whether the maker still sells the claw assembly and the gantry motor, and the fact that a well-built cabinet is genuinely a 20-year object while its PCB and coin mech are the failure points. Local colour: Singapore's claw-machine shop boom and the licensing/regulatory question that followed it — where the authorities drew the line between amusement and gaming, and what that says about payout-percentage machines. Sceptic: the honest arithmetic of "cost per win" versus just buying the plush; the Japanese ¥800 prize-value cap under the amusement business law as an example of regulation that *improved* the product; and the uncomfortable truth that the machine's fairness is a setting, not a property — so the only claw machine you can trust is one whose dip switches you control yourself.|
