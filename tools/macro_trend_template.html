The Trend · Earnings Behind The Fund
S&P 500 profit forecasts are up a third in 2026, FactSet finds
The S&P 500 trades at 17.7 times expected earnings, its cheapest since November 2023.

American companies are earning far more than analysts expected a year ago, and that, not falling share prices, is why shares now look cheaper. FactSet, the firm that collects analysts’ forecasts, said on 9 November that the profit expected over the next twelve months from the five hundred largest American companies has risen 33.6 per cent since the end of 2025. The index of their shares has risen 7.4 per cent.1

It matters to anyone holding a global share fund, because 62.83 per cent of the all-world index is American.2 The usual yardstick for a share is its price divided by one year of expected profit: how many years of earnings a buyer pays for. When profits grow faster than prices, that number falls without anybody losing money.

It has fallen a long way. The multiple was 22.0 at the end of 2025, 19.0 on 30 September and 17.7 on 5 November, the lowest since November 2023 and below its ten-year average of 19.1.1 Third-quarter profits are running 31.9 per cent above a year earlier, against 29.1 forecast on 30 September: a third straight quarter above 25 per cent.3

Two things underneath are less comfortable. First, who earns it: the seven largest technology companies grew 22.6 per cent against 32.6 for the other 493, trailing for the first time since late 2022, and results beat forecasts by just 3.1 per cent.4

Second, which yardstick. Robert Shiller’s divides the price by ten years of inflation-adjusted profit, not one forecast year, and reads 39.16 against a long-run median of 16.14.5 One trusts next year’s estimate, made at near-record margins; the other does not.6 So watch the estimate, not the price: analysts raised fourth-quarter forecasts 1.4 per cent in October, a month in which they usually cut.7

The multiple fell a fifth while the index rose.1
Inside the Fund · Ten Holdings Are 23.5 Per Cent Of It

The all-world fund holds 3,574 companies, but the United States is 62.83 per cent of it; Japan, the next largest, is 5.94. The ten largest holdings are 23.54 per cent: Apple 4.74, Nvidia 4.55, Microsoft 3.69, then Amazon, TSMC, Alphabet twice, Broadcom, Meta and Tesla. Eight of those ten lines belong to the seven companies whose profit growth now trails the rest. All as of 31 October.2

The Call · A Real-Rate Shock

The standing call is a real-rate shock: the Federal Reserve tightening into an energy supply shock. Held since No. 162. Rising profits with a falling multiple is what dearer money looks like. What would change it: the ten-year real yield closing under 2.60 per cent; no rate rise on 9 December; or the high-yield spread above 4.0 points, which would make this a growth scare. Explanation, not advice.9
